The caveat that actually matters
This 1.375¢/call is priced on a 161-second average call. Real enterprise calls run longer — and cost scales, but not the way you'd expect.
The sample dataset averages 161 seconds (2.7 min) per call. Typical enterprise care calls run 600–1,100 seconds — 4 to 7× longer. Token cost doesn't scale 4–7× with it, though: prompt tokens (the transcript text) scale with call length, but completion tokens (the 70-field structured output) are bounded by a fixed schema — capped at 5 issues and 5 products per call — so they stay roughly flat regardless of how long the call runs. Since Claude Haiku prices output at 5× input ($5.00 vs $1.00 per million tokens), that fixed completion cost anchors the total, and real cost grows sub-linearly with call length. Prompt cost is further reduced by prompt caching — the ~2,500-token system prompt is billed once per cache window (10% of the input rate on every repeat read), not once per call.
Net effect: a call 7× longer than this sample's average costs roughly 3× more to process, not 7× more — because the most expensive part of the bill (the output) barely moves.

Data quality — the honest part

172 of 217 passing every check is the success criterion — not a footnote

Most AI call-analytics tools report a number for every call the model processes, silently baking in whatever the model got wrong. This pipeline runs three deterministic, non-LLM checks on every extraction — phase-by-phase time reconciliation, a cross-check against the source recording's own timestamps, and transcript-completeness detection — before a call's numbers are allowed to touch the AHT/cost dashboard. A call that fails is excluded from aggregation and logged with a specific reason, never silently included. These aren't one-off bugs — they're the recurring constraints any team hits processing real, messy transcripts at volume. Naming them here, with a mitigation attached, is what makes the trusted 172 defensible instead of decorative.

What this means at enterprise scale
At 100,000 calls/month, ~79,300 get board-ready analytics automatically. ~20,700 get routed to review instead of quietly corrupting the numbers.
That's the actual product: not "AI reads every call," but AI reads every call and tells you which ones it isn't confident enough about to bill your decisions on. A pipeline that reports 100% is either hand-picking its examples or hiding its error rate inside the average. This one measures it, categorizes it, and gates on it — in code, at the aggregation layer, not as a caveat in a slide deck.
Why enterprises need this

Not a nice-to-have dashboard — infrastructure a CX buyer will ask for by name

Closes the blind spot resolution rate can't see
Every AI CX vendor reports a resolution number. Almost none report cost-to-serve or avoidable-contact rate next to it. This is the layer that fills that gap — on top of whichever platform is already handling the call.
Full decision traceability, not a black box
Every agent logs its decision, reasoning, evidence, and confidence to an append-only audit trail — the kind of record a regulated telecom, banking, or healthcare CX org needs to produce for internal review.
Governance enforced in code, not policy
Budget hard-stops, PII redaction before any model call, and rate limiting are running checks in the pipeline itself — not a compliance document that has to be trusted separately.
Model-agnostic economics
Extraction model, insight model, and pricing are single config values. As model pricing and quality shift — and they will — re-tuning the cost/quality tradeoff doesn't mean rewriting the pipeline.
Additive, not a rip-and-replace
It reads transcripts after the fact. It doesn't touch the call path, so it doesn't require replacing whatever voice or chat AI is already deployed.
The cost of finding out is negligible
Projected inference cost tops out under 0.7% of the monthly cost-to-serve baseline it surfaces — the economics favour just running the pilot rather than debating the estimate.
